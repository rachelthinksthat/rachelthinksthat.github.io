Undergraduate literary criticism · LSU · April 2019

“Wanting to do it is doing it”: Action and Inaction

Toni Morrison’s “Recitatif” · Louisiana State University

Toni Morrison’s short story, “Recitatif” explores friendship, race, trauma, and mental illness as they relate to personality and their effect on the choices people make in their lives. The persona people display throughout the day can differ from their unspoken thoughts or repressed memories. Oftentimes when we remember the past, difficult memories are altered to give a more pleasant tone or replaced with a completely different memory, in order to prevent trauma from crippling our lives, which forms individual identities. Toni Morrison uses large concepts like abandonment, racial tension, disabilities, and friendship to explore how memories can affect our perception of ourselves, reality, and those around us. “Recitatif” also perfectly displays how repressed emotion acts as a catalyst for tension, and can be displaced as discrimination and racism.

Children shelter themselves from undesirable circumstances by using their imagination, and finding ways to ignore the problem, which causes them to focus less on personal advancement and more on instant gratification. Two young girls coming from broken homes would typically refuse to settle down just as Twyla and Roberta, “changed beds every night and… never picked one out as our own permanent bed” (Morrison 1429). Their lack of focus on their grades displays a decision to actively punish themselves for being abandoned, possibly because the abandonment left them feeling useless, unwanted and a detriment to society and others. The girls also exuded false confidence while internalizing their fear because, “neither of us wanted the other one to know” that the other was afraid (Morrison 1430), which shows their lack of action to confront the emotions behind their actions. Each of the girls’ abandonment scarred them into keeping their wounds hidden, keeping their questions to themselves, and believing, “what had to be believed” in order to get through the day without breaking down (Morrison 1436). Morrison starts her short story with children because many of the habits and beliefs people form about the world are rooted in their childhood.

Racial identities root themselves in people during childhood; however, instead of focusing on racism itself, Morrison uses this tension to further examine how personalities are built around repressed or altered memories and experiences. Twyla and Roberta’s races are never explicitly stated, but Morrison does describe them as, “salt and pepper” and girls that are, “from a whole other race” (Morrison 1429). As people grow older their personalities shift and change based on their experiences, so do their identifications of themselves and others. When Twyla and Roberta meet in the diner, Roberta looks down on Twyla and later attributes this to, “how it was in those days: black—white” yet Twyla remembers that time as, “just the opposite…blacks were very friendly with whites in those days” (Morrison 1437). Both girls remember the incident differently, not because of their racial difference, but because at the time they were both still hurting from their trauma. Roberta and Twyla did not want to seem unhappy or fearful to the other, and reacted with dismissiveness and anger, respectively, instead of expressing their true feelings of grief for not escaping their circumstances. In the article Racial Identities Revisited, Marie Knoflíčková argues that “by removing racial identity from a central position in the friendship of the two women, she demonstrates Appiah’s conclusion about racial identities being only one of many identities one assumes in their life” (Knoflíčková 23). Morrison specifically does not focus on race and instead emphasizes the emotions behind the way people behave because race does not define our identity, but simply acts as one of them. Repressing the truth or “never talking about it to anybody” creates tension in relationships that needs to be rationalized, so instead of confronting the truth people blame the tension on other factors such as individual racial identities (Morrison 1433).

The separation of race occurs in the memories of Twyla and Roberta when they fail to remember race of the disabled woman from the orphanage, Maggie. The girls never use race against one another, which “suggests that they perceive it as an external category imposed upon them by society”, rather than using it as their sole identification of themselves and each other (Knoflíčková 24). Both women experienced trauma as children, which gave them a similar identity in the orphanage and connected them regardless of their different race, yet both felt pity for Maggie because of her condition. Morrison uses the women’s connection in trauma and separation from race to display how discrimination can occur simply between two polar identities, like someone with disabilities and someone without. Maggie acts as, “prosthetic function in the development of this sympathy” (Sklar 138). People commonly feel sympathy or guilt when exposed to mental or physically disabled people, which Morrison uses as a comparison for sympathy, jealousy, or pity between racial identities. As Twyla and Roberta grow older and are exposed to racial difference in their culture and society this sympathy becomes reassigned to Maggie’s race rather than her disability. Maggie stands as, “the subject position of African-Americans generally”, in the tension Morrison already crafted throughout her story, “and sustained presence of Maggie in the girls’/women’s memories broadens the issue of injustice toward blacks from a theoretical exercise by reminding us of the deep scars that cruelty and mistreatment leave on the victim” (Sklar 148). The girls’ deep trauma leaks into their identities and perceptions of others, and causes their focus to be displaced onto outward issues rather than their internal ones.

Once the women find stability in their lives, their friendship blossoms again like when they were children in the orphanage; however, neither woman truly understands the other. Twyla and Roberta understand, “what nobody else in the world knew—how not to ask questions”, yet their mutual agreement does little to further their relationship. Instead of outwardly discussing their issues, they consistently misinterpret each other’s intentions and motivations. For example, during their picketing war during school desegregation the women ask one another, “I wonder what made me think you were different”, indicating they never truly understood one another at all and simply projected their own feelings onto one another because of their shared backgrounds. Morrison uses Twyla and Roberta’s relationship to present, “women’s early experiences within kyriocentric institutions like St. Bonny’s as pivotal in shaping how they form relationships with one another… she also indicates the possibility of dismantling kyriocentric gender socialization when women begin to question conventional paradigms of power in their connections to one another” (Morris 161). In any relationship there are struggles for power. By using the women’s background in an oppressive environment with an authoritative leader, Big Bozo, and victimized staff members, Maggie, Morrison dissects this constant struggle for power in their unique relationship based in mutual trauma. She also connects their personal strain to power struggles in a cultural context by presenting situations where people attribute power to certain racial and physical identities. Roberta and Twyla cannot cease their battle for power, so instead of remaining relational it becomes a racial attack against one another as Roberta accuses Twyla saying, “you kicked her. We both did. You kicked a black lady who couldn’t even scream” (Morrison 1439). Oppression and domination by an entity of power causes a similar stress and tension to the feeling of oppression and domination by one race on the other. Twyla realizes that Roberta and her fight was never about race but instead about their abandonment and oppression as she thinks, “I didn’t kick her… we watched and never tried to help her and never called for help. Maggie was my dancing mother… I knew she wouldn’t scream, couldn’t—just like me” (Morrison 1440). Twyla recognizes her victim status, finally acknowledging her trauma.

Morrison uses abandonment, race, friendship, and disability to analyze how identities affect our everyday lives. She strips away all outward recognition of racism instead of addressing it overtly, in order to place the focus on the individual rather than the overarching concept. By looking at two girls/women who experienced ostracism from their family, racial differences, oppression, and difficult relationships, Morrison accurately takes a look at all of the individual aspects that shape people’s identities. She strips down big concepts into emotion, action, and intention, which makes them relatable and understandable to many who read. By taking away race people will understand that we are not defined by it, and that we share more in how we think, feel, and experience reality than our positions in its hierarchy suggest.
