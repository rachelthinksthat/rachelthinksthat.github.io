Think piece · Documentary analysis · LSU · February 2016

Hungry for Change: Informative but BORING

English 2000 · Louisiana State University

Healthy living concerns people from all countries, cultures, races, and sexes. However most people have no idea what healthy living even consists of. Many doctors and health experts try to inform people about how to live a healthy life by putting up websites, giving lectures, making documentaries, etc. Health documentaries show the struggles people face in every day life, and reveal the addiction people never knew existed. They engage the audience in order to make the reliable information understandable and also interesting. The Documentary Hungry for Change means to inspire people lost in their food addictions, and teach them how to control their health and their life. Hungry for Change uses expert opinions to clearly explain healthy living; however, the never-ending lectures, terrible music, and awful acting overshadow the reliable information and make the documentary dreadful.

Evidence used in documentaries comes from reliable sources experienced in the issue being discussed. Hungry for change gathered health journalists, health experts, doctors, naturalists, and more to reveal the crushing truth about the food in today’s society. Each of the speakers spent years in Health Community studying what the body needs to be healthy, and many have had personal struggles with health. They use real examples of people who used the wrong diets to make themselves “healthy”, along with supporting their information using science. Jon Gabriel, an author and Weight Loss Expert, references research scientists have done with msg. For example, he references a study done on obesity using mice that consume msg, to explain why msg makes people gain weight. The experts discuss a variety of topics regarding health, each supporting the other’s statements. For instance, when talking about how to cleanse the body Jon Gabriel says, “you have to address the fact that you’re being assaulted with toxins” and Jason Vale, author and addiction specialist, says, “the body is crying out to heal… it wants to naturally detoxify” and also Daniel Vitalis, a Traditional and Wild Foods expert, says, “probably the best thing a person can do in the beginning is clean themselves out probably get on a detoxification program.” These trustworthy experts use reliable information to inform the audience on how to have a healthy life.

Information in documentaries clearly and understandably relays the message to the audience. Many of the tactics Hungry For Change uses make the information being presented clear and understandable to the audience. The documentary supplies the audience with images of what the speaker talks about, like showing broccoli when they refer to chlorophyll rich foods, to make concepts easily remembered. They use some ads like the Corn Refiners Assoc Ad, which talks about how good corn syrup is for you, to emphasize how misleading the distributers are. In-between interviews, quotes will fade in occasionally to reinforce the information just stated. For example, after a doctor explains how scientists develop chemicals to make food taste good, they use a quote saying, “msg and free glutamates are used to enhance flavor in about 80% of all processed foods. – Raymond Francis M. Sc. MIT.” When experts explain a process, like how the body becomes addicted to cigarettes, an animation helps the explanation by showing the audience what happens. History about how cave men used to scavenge for food, and go days without nourishment helps people understand why the body still thinks it needs to store fat for times of famine, rather than use fat like it uses micronutrients. By breaking down large science concepts into bite sized bits, Hungry for Change successfully provides clear and easy to understand information to the audience.

Documentaries should engage and intrigue the audience, in order to successfully relay the purpose of the video. They should have good video and audio quality, and have enough flair to keep the audience’s attention. Hungry for Change prevails as the least captivating documentary ever made. The inclusion of expert opinions may be necessary for a documentary to be reliable, however the documentary Hungry for Change uses too many lectures making it painful to watch. Switching from one lecturer to another talking about the same exact thing for three minutes, then to another, and on and on for the entire video is overkill. Twenty experts talking about how sugar kills you for thirty minutes does not engage people who are not already interested in nutrition. The information about what types of fat are good for you and bad for you seems interesting, aside from the fierce lectures every twelve seconds. Each and every one of the speakers make food addiction seem pressing by using forceful tones, but after the tenth time the audience will be screaming “WE GET IT ALREADY!” Hungry for Change does provide good video and audio quality, which happens to be the only engaging thing about it. The terrible and repetitive music takes the focus away from, “hmm this video seems interesting…” and puts it on, “why the hell would they use this depressing music?” The documentary tries to display struggles people have with food by following a young woman’s ‘story’. The horrific acting in every scene accompanies a lame story line about a girl who mopes around her crappy life eating crappy food, and then miraculously becomes happy when she starts being healthy. The actress stares at the ingredients on a diet coke bottle and then looks off into the distance as she decides to drink it anyway… are they SERIOUS? No one wants to watch a girl on a diet longingly stare at a pizza box! For some reason the editors thought it would be a good idea to include the character’s crush on a coworker, which is just unnecessary and lame. The audience won’t even be able to finish Hungry for Change because they will fall asleep from boredom.

Hungry for change may present a clear and sound argument for healthy living, but the infinite lectures, atrocious music, and horrific acting kill any chance of it being interesting. If a person finds nutrition interesting and wants to know valid information about food and nutrients, then this documentary is perfect for them, that is if they can stand to watch thirty seconds of it. Had the documentary producers spent more time on forming a story, putting together animations, choosing lively music, and removing a few of the repetitive interviews, then it would be engaging as well as informative. The documentary does a good job simplifying tough scientific concepts and using reliable sources, however, the documentary’s lack of intrigue kills any chance of it getting its point across. Hungry for Change may be trustworthy, but nowadays people crave entertainment not monotonous lectures.
