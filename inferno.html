Inferno: two prototypes

A collectible card deck and a modern dual translation of Dante's Inferno, with my critical reflection. Inspired by my undergraduate reading of Dante at LSU.

Two prototypes

Prototype 1, Inferno Anatomy, is a collectible deck of cards for tourists and museum goers who want a unique aesthetic memento of the Inferno. Prototype 2 is a new modern dual translation for college age collectors who enjoy poetry and want an eye-catching book that's easy to carry around and use for class.

Inspiration: reading Dante at LSU

My undergraduate study of Dante’s Inferno at Louisiana State University was the inspiration for this MA project. Asked why it is truer to depict Hell as freezing rather than burning, I wrote:

“Freezing causes much more suffering than burning does because it creeps slowly and causes them to stay suspended in their torment, unable to take action. A fire can be put out quickly but ice takes much longer to melt, so there is this sense of an inability to escape from the torment.”

Critical rationale

The highly regarded epic poem Dante’s Divine Comedy has many enthusiasts who would be thrilled to stop at an exhibit on Dante himself or even take a trip to Italy to deepen their experience with the literature. Therefore, my first prototype targets these enthusiasts by highlighting itself as a memento to remind them of their experiences with the content. Using this logic, I chose to create collectible cards that would give them a higher sense of interaction and involvement with the text as a new and unique representation that reinvigorates their passion for the Inferno through colorful visually striking imagery. The purchaser finds themselves emotionally drawn in by the striking colors combined with haunting visual imagery of twisted hands clawing them into the dead souls’ tragic eternal punishment. Cards also have an uncertainty about them, and the purchaser may even decide to create their own game using this set as an impressive conversation starter. After all, you never know what sin may afflict you next. My design also needed to be striking enough for displaying on a shelf or coffee table as a piece of intrigue. Choosing to include the canto each section was pulled from allows for use as a reference companion, a sneaky way to get them to rationalize the purchase to themselves.

Dante’s Divine Comedy, specifically the Inferno, has traditionally been critically reviewed and studied by researchers, educators, students, and other readers since the 1300s. I personally was introduced to the content in high school and later again in university, so I felt a new modern translated copy with aesthetic appeal that students could carry around easily would be a good option for my second prototype. The poem’s a bit dense, so, a lightweight book with a unique design and wonky handwritten text may catch the eye of a sixteen to twenty-two year old. Leaving extra space in the margin and the original English translation for comparison allows for these young adults to use the text for actual study if they’d like to, and would stand out amongst their peers’ dull copies of the text.

Decision making

In class, when we were giving each other peer feedback, my classmate mentioned to me that one of the images I was using for inspiration looked like a card face, and I had a total a-ha moment that I wanted to create some sort of card deck. GF Smith’s sustainability practices in their papermaking were the reason why I chose to work with their paper. I focused on color theory as my main medium of communication, and my progress for Prototype One as a whole progressed exponentially after deciding to focus on color and imagery rather than form.

The main decisions for the second prototype revolved around how large to make my text, the size of the book itself, and how wide the margins should be to allow for notetaking. Printing mockups helped solidify all of these questions into concrete answers because holding pages in your hands gives you more of an idea of what the final product would look like than just looking at a design on the computer screen. When we went to the publishing fair, I found books with Munken paper and decided to use it for my second prototype after speaking with the man at the booth, who told me it was great for writing on and made sustainably.

What I’d do differently

As I worked with my tutor to create the mockup for the cards with concentric circle cutouts in InDesign, it became clear I needed to scrap the idea. I was too focused on logistics, which took my focus off looking for other ways to create prototypes that would reach my audiences. Successfully learning how to manipulate images into my envisioned end product on Photoshop gave me confidence and a drive to move forward.

I’m satisfied with the imagery I chose because the hands used throughout, especially on the front card, feel like they would make someone want to pick them up if they saw them on a shelf in a museum or tourist shop. The color design came out beautifully and works well as a whole. I should have put more effort into designing a box for the cards to go in instead of choosing to simply tie them with a red ribbon, and into understanding what typeface would be the most effective for easy reading. For Prototype Two, I could have translated the text myself instead of using AI, which would’ve made the prototype more creative and personal.

Publishing and sustainability

Both of the paper types and ribbon fastener I chose to use come from companies that prioritize sustainable processes in their products (Arctic Paper, Tiny Box Company, GF Smith). I aimed at demonstrating that sustainably used materials can still be cost effective. Digital printing in a small press also lowers the costs through short run productions and makes reprints have a quicker turnaround.

Prototype One will fit into the tourism and museum market, and would be best for small print production to give the added appeal of limited edition uniqueness to collectors. People may buy it as a gift, so having shorter runs around high purchasing times will make the cost more effective. Prototype Two fits into a mid-sized trade press focused on reimagined classics.
