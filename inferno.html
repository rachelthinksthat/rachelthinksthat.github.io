Inferno: two prototypes

A collectible card deck and a modern dual translation of Dante's Inferno, with my critical reflection. Inspired by my undergraduate reading of Dante at LSU.

Two prototypes

Prototype 1, Inferno Anatomy, is a collectible deck of cards for tourists and museum goers who want a unique aesthetic memento of the Inferno. Prototype 2 is a new modern dual translation for college age collectors who enjoy poetry and want an eye-catching book that's easy to carry around and use for class.

Inspiration: reading Dante at LSU

My undergraduate study of Dante’s Inferno at Louisiana State University was the inspiration for this MA project. These are two of my answers from that class, written on Cantos 31–34.

Why is it truer to depict Hell as freezing rather than burning?

Hell being cold could be a metaphor of God’s warmth and goodness, because furthest depths in hell his warmth cannot be reached at all and those sinners have no hope of redemption. For lesser sins people can feel a smaller part of God until the greatest of all sins, which is the betrayal of God himself, where there is no warmth and Satan is simply encased in a huge block of ice. Also, freezing causes much more suffering than burning does because it creeps slowly and causes them to stay suspended in their torment, unable to take action. A fire can be put out quickly but ice takes much longer to melt, so there is this sense of an inability to escape from the torment.

Other than Geryon, what image or symbol would you pick to personify hell?

I would pick an archway with a reflection pool stretching between the two legs of the arch. The archway stands tall and looks magnificently crafted and as if it is of the lord, seemingly full of power and wealth for people. However the pool really acts as a guard, and contains a beast with terrible talons ready to rip people down into hell. The pool would reflect a man’s greatest desires, and show their darkest secrets. If a man were to look into the reflection and see their fleshly desires and walk toward them then they would be sucked into the archway by these long taloned hands, which would emerge at the presence of a sinner and thrust them into hell.

Sin is appealing and the archway lures people in by seemingly feeding people what they desperately wish for without having to worship anyone else for it.

Critical rationale

The highly regarded epic poem Dante’s Divine Comedy has many enthusiasts who would be thrilled to stop at an exhibit on Dante himself or even take a trip to Italy to deepen their experience with the literature. Therefore, my first prototype targets these enthusiasts by highlighting itself as a memento to remind them of their experiences with the content. Using this logic, I chose to create collectible cards that would give them a higher sense of interaction and involvement with the text as a new and unique representation that reinvigorates their passion for the Inferno through colorful visually striking imagery. The purchaser finds themselves emotionally drawn in by the striking colors combined with haunting visual imagery of twisted hands clawing them into the dead souls’ tragic eternal punishment. Cards also have an uncertainty about them, and the purchaser may even decide to create their own game using this set as an impressive conversation starter. After all, you never know what sin may afflict you next. My design also needed to be striking enough for displaying on a shelf or coffee table as a piece of intrigue. Choosing to include the canto each section was pulled from allows for use as a reference companion, a sneaky way to get them to rationalize the purchase to themselves.

Dante’s Divine Comedy, specifically the Inferno, has traditionally been critically reviewed and studied by researchers, educators, students, and other readers since the 1300s. I personally was introduced to the content in high school and later again in university, so I felt a new modern translated copy with aesthetic appeal that students could carry around easily would be a good option for my second prototype. The poem’s a bit dense, so, a lightweight book with a unique design and wonky handwritten text may catch the eye of a sixteen to twenty-two year old. Leaving extra space in the margin and the original English translation for comparison allows for these young adults to use the text for actual study if they’d like to, and would stand out amongst their peers’ dull copies of the text.

Decision making

In the beginning, my two audiences and my lightweight modern translation design for the second prototype were clear and straightforward. However, the form for the first prototype was vastly circulating through many different complex collectible styles, but I had solidified my choice to represent each circle of hell uniquely. In class, when we were giving each other peer feedback, Anushka mentioned to me that one of the images I was using for inspiration looked like a card face, and I had a total a-ha moment that I wanted to create some sort of card deck. She also helped me decide to use symbols to represent each layer visually. Then, when we had the GF smith guest lecturer, I decided I saw the sample, shown in the figure to the right, that made me hell bent on creating a concentric circle design with cutouts within each card.

GF Smith’s sustainability practices in their papermaking were also the reason why I chose to work with their paper. I also decided to use a 10mm wide sustainable grosgrain recycled black ribbon to secure them together (Tiny Box Company). During my time researching translations for the second prototype, I found the original illustrations in Rev. H.F. Cary’s translation and decided to use them as my card faces, and focused on color theory as my main medium of communication and found a website on color psychology to use as a basis for choosing colors for each sin circle (ColorPsychology.org). I’m glad I researched color and used it for the focus of Prototype One because the design in the end benefited from my effort, and my progress for Prototype One as a whole progressed exponentially after deciding to focus on color and imagery rather than form.

The main decisions for the second prototype revolved around how large to make my text, the size of the book itself, and how wide the margins should be to allow for notetaking. Printing mockups helped solidify all of these questions into concrete answers because holding pages in your hands gives you more of an idea of what the final product would look like than just looking at a design on the computer screen. I think more of my efforts for Prototype Two were focused on paper type because I wanted paper that could be written on with ease and was opaque enough so written notes would not bleed through. When we went to the publishing fair, I found books with munken paper and decided to use it for my second prototype after speaking with the man at the booth, who told me it was great for writing on and made sustainably. In retrospect, I really should’ve put more effort into understanding what typeface would be the most effective for easy reading because I truthfully just chose one at random.

Problems and challenges

My ideas were clear as day to me as we first began the project, but unfortunately my vision was way outside of my design capabilities. Looking back, I would have reckoned with myself, considered my lack of skill, and chosen a simpler text to work with like The Adventures of Winnie the Pooh. As I worked with Deshna to create the mockup for the cards with concentric circle cutouts in InDesign, it became clear I was simply not capable of executing it and needed to scrap the idea. I was really obstinate and truly self sabotaging with my frustration for using InDesign, and my lack of flexible thinking held me back from moving forward with the project. I was too focused on logistics, which took away from my experience and took my focus off looking for other ways to create prototypes that would reach my audiences. After Deshna helped me set everything up, receiving peer assistance for issues I had along the way, and consistently receiving reassurance from both, I felt a bit better, and that’s when I was able to focus more on the visual aspects of Prototype One rather than the form. Successfully learning how to manipulate images into my envisioned end product on Photoshop also gave me confidence and a drive to move forward. There were few challenges with Prototype Two because the design was straightforward. Most of the challenges for the second prototype also had to do with InDesign, and were also resolved with help from Deshna.

Outcomes

Prototype One came out as well as it could have with my skillset. I’m satisfied with the imagery I chose because the hands used throughout, especially on the front card, feel like they would make someone want to pick them up if they saw them on a shelf in a museum or tourist shop. Making the hand imagery the focal point of the prototype evokes a more tactile personal feel, leaning into the collectible aspect I was initially aiming to produce. I do wish I could have found a way to make the physical form more interesting for the prototype. The color design came out beautifully and works well as a whole. I should have put more effort into designing a box for the cards to go in instead of choosing to simply tie them with a red ribbon.

The main regret I have for Prototype Two is choosing to use AI for translating the original English translation into modern language. I think I could have found a better way to translate the text because, looking back, my choice seems pretty lazy. I’m a strong writer and could have translated the text myself, which would’ve made the prototype more creative and personal. I do think the final product would be picked up at a bookstore by a young adult who wanted a copy of Dante’s Inferno, but probably not for education purposes and more for aesthetic collector’s appeal purposes.

Publishing and sustainability

Broadly, my considerations in the wider context of publishing focused more on sustainability and cost effectiveness. Both of the paper types and ribbon fastener I chose to use come from companies that prioritize sustainable processes in their products (Arctic Paper) (Tiny Box Company) (GF Smith). I aimed at demonstrating that sustainably used materials can still be cost effective, but I have not determined the total production costs and am going off of the price of the materials alone. Overall, I do think the flexibility of the prototypes and their likelihood of success in the market adds to the rationality for production costs.

Considering our lecture on print production, I found that digital printing in a small press also lowers the costs through short run productions and makes reprints have a quicker turnaround (University of the Arts London). The aesthetic card deck, Prototype One, will fit into the tourism and museum market, and would be best for small print production to give the added appeal of limited edition uniqueness to collectors. People may buy it as a gift, so having shorter runs around high purchasing times will make the cost more effective. Also, using ribbons as the fastening means there will be a manual aspect to production, so a small press would be more reasonable. The dual translation aimed at young adults, Prototype Two, turned out to be more niche than I initially intended since it only reimagines the Inferno portion of The Divine Comedy rather than the entire thing, which gives it more of a collector’s edition appeal. Therefore, Prototype Two fits into a mid-sized trade press focused on reimagined classics best considering its market capability has a smaller range than major trade market publishers expect.
