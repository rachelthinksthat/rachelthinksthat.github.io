Anti-Feminism in The Wife of Bath’s Tale and Prologue

ENGL 4137 · Louisiana State University

Women in the middle ages had little rights compared to women in present times. Much of women’s rights in the middle ages had to do with their place in society and religious roles they played. Class was a greater defining factor than sex was at that time; however, there was still division between men’s and women’s rights in all of the classes. Women could educate themselves and increase their literacy, in some circumstances they could hold power and lands, and they could master certain crafts, such as spinning tapestries. Women were expected to submit to their husbands in marriage, and were only allowed to separate or divorce if they suspected adultery. Peasants had less authority in their lives than women of higher class, and were completely under the rule of their husbands and lords. Although the idea of women being sovereign over their husbands makes The Wife of Bath’s Tale and Prologue seem like proto-feminist literature, Alisoun’s use of her sexuality to manipulate men furthers stereotypes of women and discredits her as a feminist figure.

Pro-Feminist themes in The Wife of Bath’s Tale and Prologue include, Alison’s literacy, courage, and shamelessness while telling her tale, and the idea of women having authority throughout the tale. In the Prologue Alisoun unabashedly and with no authority tells the group of her experience, “Experience, though noon auctoritee/Were in this world, is right ynogh for me/To speke of wo that is in mariage;” (lines 1-3). Alisoun does not care what people say, and will do as she wishes in the way of marriage, “Bet is to be wedded than to brynne./What rekketh me, thogh folk seye vileynye/Of shrewed Lameth and his bigamye?” (lines 52-54). Alisoun has had five husbands and thinks she has done nothing wrong in the eyes of the Lord because men like Solomon were allowed many wives. Alisoun quotes scripture and literature in the prologue to her tale in order to show her knowledge and credibility to the men in her presence, “And seye thise wordes in the Apostles name:/"In habit maad with chastitee and shame/Ye wommen shul apparaille yow," quod he,” (line 341-343). She attempts to give herself an advantage over men in order to reinforce her idea that women deserve to have sovereignty over their husbands; which she uses as the main idea of The Wife of Bath’s Tale. By ‘sovereignty’ she simply means the right for women to choose things for themselves, instead of constantly being controlled by their husbands. She places women in control at all points in the story by giving the Queen power over the Knight’s punishment, giving the loathly woman the answer the knight searches for, and having the Knight relinquish his final choice to the loathly lady. Alisoun’s courage and knowledge in telling her prologue and her use of women in authority in her tale, make her seem like a feminist hero of her time; she seems to stand up to men and tell them what women want.

However, Alisoun’s emphasis on her use of sexuality to control her husband depletes her voice as a feminist hero, and instead makes her fall into the stereotype that women are only good for their bodies. Lines 400-418 in the prologue detail Alisoun’s withhold or use of sex in order to manipulate her husbands to give into her desires. She says, “I wolde no lenger in the bed abyde,…/ Til he had maad his raunson unto me;/Thanne wolde I suffre hym do his nycetee” (lines 409-412). Even when Alisoun gains sovereignty from her fifth husband, whom she loved most, she deceives him by pretending to be dead so that he relinquishes his authority over her, “And that he seyde, ‘Myn owene trewe wyf,/Do as thee lust the terme of al thy lyf;/Keep thyn honour, and keep eek myn estaat’” (lines 819-821). This act of deceit does nothing to promote women’s right to sovereignty, or choice, and simply labels them as manipulative and deceitful. Yes her husband abused her mentally, by reading the “book of wikked wyves” (line 685), and physically “with his fest he smoot me on the heed” (line 795); however, instead of going off and finding her independence apart from her husband, she chose to deceive him into staying with her and giving her authority. Had she left him and used the land she acquired from her other husbands and lived on her own power and means, then she would have been a credible feminist figure. Considering that women of the time could have reign over their own land, especially if they were of higher class, which Alisoun had to have been if she was literate and knowledgeable, the idea of Alisoun living by her own means is not unfounded. Using her body for her bidding does not make her independent from her husband, and simply gives her a false sense of authority over him. She does not reason with her husbands or prove her worth to them, and instead reduces herself to an object for their gratification that they only need to pay in order to receive.

In addition, the men listening to her tale seem as though they are not interested in the slightest, and Alisoun even asks for permission to continue on. The friar at the end of Alisoun’s prologue laughs at how long it was, “The Frere lough, whan he hadde herd al this;” (line 829). Being a man of the church a friar should understand that men and women are to have a mutual respect of each other in marriage; however, he laughs at her prologue perhaps because of the length or perhaps because he finds the idea of women having authority to choose ludicrous. The length of The Wife of Bath’s Prologue, stands out against others in Chaucer’s collection of tales, and could indicate that Chaucer wants the wife’s prologue to resemble a manipulation tactic. The length of her prologue drones on and leaves off with her mention of gaining sovereignty over her husband, which is immediately followed by the friar’s laughter. The closeness of these two happenings suggests that the idea of women having a choice is laughable. After the Summoner and the Friar’s brief interlude the Wife of Bath asks the friar, “If I have licence of this worthy Frere."/"Yis, dame," quod he, "tel forth, and I wol heere." (lines 855-856). Asking the friar to continue with her story may have been a joke at the friar’s expense since he was interrupting her; however considering the possible feminist tones of the text, it goes against the idea of women having power or authority. Instead of asking the friar for permission to continue, Alisoun could have simply said that she was going to continue, and not have given the friar power in that moment. Asking for permission from a man, would not have occurred from a true feminist figure, and so Alisoun’s actions further discredit her from truly standing for women’s justice.

Although The Wife of Bath’s Tale and Prologue have some feminist tones, the overall story does not promote feminism, and continues to push women into stereotypes. Alisoun holds feminist ideas and truly wishes for women to have the authority to make their own choices in marriage; yet, she still submits to men consistently and refuses to take total control over her own life. She makes herself into an object for men’s affections, and continues to remarry, trapping herself, even though she considers herself as this powerful and intelligent woman. In the end, Chaucer’s Tale and Prologue from The Wife of Bath hold some feminist ideas that are never truly developed because a falsely independent woman tells them.
