Documentary analysis · English 2000 · February 2016

Hungry for Change: Informative but BORING

Healthy living concerns people from all countries, cultures, races, and sexes. However most people have no idea what healthy living even consists of. Many doctors and health experts try to inform people about how to live a healthy life by putting up websites, giving lectures, making documentaries, etc. Health documentaries show the struggles people face in every day life, and reveal the addiction people never knew existed. They engage the audience in order to make the reliable information understandable and also interesting. The Documentary Hungry for Change means to inspire people lost in their food addictions, and teach them how to control their health and their life. Hungry for Change uses expert opinions to clearly explain healthy living; however, the never-ending lectures, terrible music, and awful acting overshadow the reliable information and make the documentary dreadful.

Evidence used in documentaries comes from reliable sources experienced in the issue being discussed. Hungry for change gathered health journalists, health experts, doctors, naturalists, and more to reveal the crushing truth about the food in today’s society. Each of the speakers spent years in Health Community studying what the body needs to be healthy, and many have had personal struggles with health. They use real examples of people who used the wrong diets to make themselves “healthy”, along with supporting their information using science. Jon Gabriel, an author and Weight Loss Expert, references research scientists have done with msg. For example, he references a study done on obesity using mice that consume msg, to explain why msg makes people gain weight. The experts discuss a variety of topics regarding health, each supporting the other’s statements. For instance, when talking about how to cleanse the body Jon Gabriel says, “you have to address the fact that you’re being assaulted with toxins” and Jason Vale, author and addiction specialist, says, “the body is crying out to heal… it wants to naturally detoxify” and also Daniel Vitalis, a Traditional and Wild Foods expert, says, “probably the best thing a person can do in the beginning is clean themselves out probably get on a detoxification program.” These trustworthy experts use reliable information to inform the audience on how to have a healthy life.

Information in documentaries clearly and understandably relays the message to the audience. Many of the tactics Hungry For Change uses make the information being presented clear and understandable to the audience. The documentary supplies the audience with images of what the speaker talks about, like showing broccoli when they refer to chlorophyll rich foods, to make concepts easily remembered. They use some ads like the Corn Refiners Assoc Ad, which talks about how good corn syrup is for you, to emphasize how misleading the distributers are. In-between interviews, quotes will fade in occasionally to reinforce the information just stated. For example, after a doctor explains how scientists develop chemicals to make food taste good, they use a quote saying, “msg and free glutamates are used to enhance flavor in about 80% of all processed foods. – Raymond Francis M. Sc. MIT.” When experts explain a process, like how the body becomes addicted to cigarettes, an animation helps the explanation by showing the audience what happens. History about how cave men used to scavenge for food, and go days without nourishment helps people understand why the body still thinks it needs to store fat for times of famine, rather than use fat like it uses micronutrients. By breaking down large science concepts into bite sized bits, Hungry for Change successfully provides clear and easy to understand information to the audience.

Documentaries should engage and intrigue the audience, in order to successfully relay the purpose of the video. They should have good video and audio quality, and have enough flair to keep the audience’s attention. Hungry for Change prevails as the least captivating documentary ever made. The inclusion of expert opinions may be necessary for a documentary to be reliable, however the documentary Hungry for Change uses too many lectures making it painful to watch. Switching from one lecturer to another talking about the same exact thing for three minutes, then to another, and on and on for the entire video is overkill. Twenty experts talking about how sugar kills you for thirty minutes does not engage people who are not already interested in nutrition. The information about what types of fat are good for you and bad for you seems interesting, aside from the fierce lectures every twelve seconds. Each and every one of the speakers make food addiction seem pressing by using forceful tones, but after the tenth time the audience will be screaming “WE GET IT ALREADY!” Hungry for Change does provide good video and audio quality, which happens to be the only engaging thing about it. The terrible and repetitive music takes the focus away from, “hmm this video seems interesting…” and puts it on, “why the hell would they use this depressing music?” The documentary tries to display struggles people have with food by following a young woman’s ‘story’. The horrific acting in every scene accompanies a lame story line about a girl who mopes around her crappy life eating crappy food, and then miraculously becomes happy when she starts being healthy. The actress stares at the ingredients on a diet coke bottle and then looks off into the distance as she decides to drink it anyway… are they SERIOUS? No one wants to watch a girl on a diet longingly stare at a pizza box! For some reason the editors thought it would be a good idea to include the character’s crush on a coworker, which is just unnecessary and lame. The audience won’t even be able to finish Hungry for Change because they will fall asleep from boredom.

Hungry for change may present a clear and sound argument for healthy living, but the infinite lectures, atrocious music, and horrific acting kill any chance of it being interesting. If a person finds nutrition interesting and wants to know valid information about food and nutrients, then this documentary is perfect for them, that is if they can stand to watch thirty seconds of it. Had the documentary producers spent more time on forming a story, putting together animations, choosing lively music, and removing a few of the repetitive interviews, then it would be engaging as well as informative. The documentary does a good job simplifying tough scientific concepts and using reliable sources, however, the documentary’s lack of intrigue kills any chance of it getting its point across. Hungry for Change may be trustworthy, but nowadays people crave entertainment not monotonous lectures.

Advertisement analysis · English 1001 · September 2014

CVS/Pharmacy's Genius Fame Stealing Advertisement

The CVS/Pharmacy advertisement in People Magazine displays several brand name products, Crest 3D Whitening strips, Olay Moisturizer, Venus Snap razor, and Cover Girl Mascara, along with two girls, a hashtag which connects the ad to the social media app, Twitter, and an MTV Video Music Awards (VMA) sponsored logo. The advertisement uses a collage-like formatting with girls posed in the bottom left corner and the products floating up and to the right of them. On the top of the advertisement in large words reads “MAKE THE GRADE WITH Chart-Topping Trends Celeb-worthy looks seen at the VMAs send you back to campus in style” (CVS/Pharmacy Advertisement, People). In the bottom right corner of the advertisement lays the CVS/Pharmacy logo. The CVS/Pharmacy advertisement in People Magazine uses the desirability of certain brand name products to increase their own popularity, two flawless-looking girls that entice the audience by appealing to their vanities and envy using the window effect, and eye-catching words and colors that pique the audience’s interest, to make customers flock to discover the convenience and variety within CVS/Pharmacy.

Different brand name products associated with beauty, such as Crest, Olay, Cover Girl, and Venus, are displayed on the CVS/Pharmacy advertisement, which advertises the products and gives credibility to the store’s endless supply of necessities. It may seem as though the use of other products’ popularity is cheating in the advertisement world, but for a convenience store it is genius. By associating themselves with popular personal appearance products, CVS/Pharmacy increases their own popularity and desirability. The products chosen, such as whitening strips, moisturizer, mascara, and a razor, appeal to the viewer’s constant need for attractiveness. All of these products were selected to be displayed because they are expendable and considered necessities to the public. Displaying the products on their advertisement will make the viewer associate the products with CVS/Pharmacy, which as a result will cause the viewer to subliminally choose CVS/Pharmacy the next time they purchase that product.

The personal appearance products are accompanied by two gorgeous girls posed in a modelesque manner, which make the viewer self-conscious and overtake them with envy. The girls in the advertisement employ window effect by showing the audience what they could become. The way that the girls pose makes their physique look effortless, and as today’s world calls it, ‘hipster’. The girls are young and carrying books which relates to vain college girls who are fretting about their next look of the day and who still need to purchase school supplies. The books they carry add to CVS/Pharmacy’s wide array of products supplied within the store, and further captivate College girls’ attention with a future need. CVS/Pharmacy uses the girls in the advertisement to show that the result of coming in to the store and purchasing their products is flawlessness and preparedness.

Above the flawless girls are huge pink and black words that ignite the need for beauty even further; even the hashtag suggests an attainable “rocking” look and the VMAs advertisement implies that the viewer has an entourage which gives them a sense of importance. The usage of words such as “Classic”, “Chart-Topping”, “Campus Bombshell”, and “Celeb-worthy” all suggest that the viewer will be the envy of all others if they purchase products from CVS/Pharmacy. The colors, pink and black, were specifically chosen because pink is associated with girls and black is mysterious. The aforementioned words often identify important and beautiful people, which will captivate the viewer’s interest. The Twitter hashtag spreads the word about CVS/Pharmacy’s large supply of products that give you #LOOKSTHATROCK. Using the hashtag is both a good advertising strategy and grabs the interest of girls who use Twitter. The VMAs advertisement appeals to the girls need for importance and fame and also gives credibility to CVS/Pharmacy as if to say their products make people VMA ready.

The techniques used in the CVS/Pharmacy advertisement in People Magazine including memorable words and ties to Twitter and the VMAs, brand name products, and flawless girls increase CVS/Pharmacy’s credibility and reliability for a variety of products. The advertisement makes the store memorable and draws in college girls who want to be noticed. The advertisement convinces college girls to associate the beauty they strive for with CVS/Pharmacy, and as the need for flawlessness arises, they will remember where they can go to improve their image.

Media literacy paper · Mass Communications 2000 · February 2015

Net Neutrality on Last Week Tonight with John Oliver

Recently on Last Week Tonight with John Oliver the topic of Net Neutrality was discussed. John Oliver is the English host of Last Week Tonight with John Oliver on HBO, similar to The Daily Show with Jon Stewart; he says he has, “full creative freedom” of the show since there are no commercial interruptions1. The show takes a satirical look, which it normally does, at Net Neutrality. The Huffington Post says he has, “the trait Stewart's and Colbert's programs lack: perspective. Oliver has the advantage of seven-day analysis”2. The show has time to listen to the buzz and recent updates in order to prepare their story. They choose satire to simplify topics that may be hard to understand, in order to spread concern for pressing issues in society.

Last Week Tonight with John Oliver airs at 10 PM CT on HBO giving it a broad audience, aside from adolescents. People pay to watch HBO and be entertained, which makes it likely for them to watch a comical news show instead of a regular one. The same people watching are paying for their Internet, which makes them personally affected by Net Neutrality and likely to watch a segment about it. When Oliver uses “we” addressing the audience it makes them see the situation as it relates to them. People who watch regular news might see the story as a ridiculous interpretation of what is actually going on. However, people with a good sense of humor may see this as a call to action; especially when Oliver asks the crazy commenters on the Internet to give it all they’ve got on the FCC’s comment page3. People who may not have heard about this issue may especially be affected by his call to action, because it is a way they can let out what they are feeling about the issue.

The news stories that appear on Last Week Tonight with John Oliver have significance and should be spread to the public at large. Distributing news as entertainment intrigues people about what is happening around them, and makes stories that could have just been simple news go viral4. Every time the show makes another seemingly dull story go viral or spurs viewers to action, it receives publicity and views. The show is comical journalism that does not focus on one area of society; it is focused on whatever story consumed America the previous week5. This particular story is primarily political, and was spurred forth because of the recent actions of the FCC and cable companies, trying to make the Internet a two-tiered system.

John Oliver uses funny lines that don’t relate to his topic, and yet he still gets people to think about stories that would have otherwise been lost in the sea of boring news. He explains the problem the Internet faces from the potential loss of net neutrality using evidence from several different sources6. He describes the cable companies, FCC, activists, and corporations with a bias for the Internet remaining neutral instead of becoming two tiered. The bias leaves little room for a viewer to see the other side of the argument. Bashing those who stand for the two tiered Internet makes their argument seem ridiculous, and convinces people to go to the net neutrality side of the argument.

The Net Neutrality story on Last Week Tonight with John Oliver was a success, and even ended up crashing the FCC’s comment section. The funny story relates to the audience and helps them understand what exactly Net Neutrality is and why it should be talked about.
