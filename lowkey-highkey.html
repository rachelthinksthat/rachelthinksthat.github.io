A Study of the Relationship Between 'lowkey' and 'highkey'

Original survey of 23 people plus syntax analysis.

Introduction

Highkey is an adverb that has been developing the past couple of years as an opposite of the term Lowkey. The earliest publicly documented usage of the term 'highkey' in opposition to 'lowkey' occurs in a song titled "Only" by Nicki Minaj in a line uttered by the rapper Drake: "Yeah, low key it may be high key." In the line, Lowkey is used to describe Drake's aforementioned action as secretive, and Highkey is used to admit that the aforementioned action may have been obvious. I will report on the correlation between the two terms using survey data based off of 23 participants, and provide analysis using Stacey Stanfield and Kristen Thomas' analysis on the syntax of Lowkey and literature related to the subject.

In Stanfield and Thomas' data, they shared lowkey in various adjective placements with the meaning 'not elaborate, showy, or intensive; modest or restrained.' Highkey acts as a contrasting adjective to lowkey with the meaning 'elaborate, showy, intensive; immodest or unrestrained; entertaining.'

  1. We had a highkey Mardi Gras weekend (intense)
  2. I'm having a party but I want to keep it highkey (entertaining)
  3. That dress is a good choice, it's very highkey (showy)
  4. This is a highkey party. (elaborate)

According to Stanfield and Thomas, as an adverb lowkey means 'secretly or discreetly' or 'slightly or kinda.' Highkey also acts in opposition to lowkey as an adverb that can mean 'obviously or indiscreetly' or 'really or very'.

  1. Highkey, those cookies are really good. (obviously)
  2. Lowkey, those cookies are really good. (secretly)
  3. I highkey want to go to that party. (really)
  4. I lowkey want to go to that party. (kinda)

The survey

Both terms have multiple meanings in different placements of the sentence. In my survey when respondents, with no explanation of meaning, were asked to define the terms highkey and lowkey, most respondents placed the words in opposition with one another. Some notable responses were as follows.

  • 'Highkey seems like your very obvious with something, while lowkey is not wanting everyone to know.'
  • 'Highkey means noticeable and lowkey means surprising and not noticeable.'
  • 'Highkey is sharing the opinion with everyone and lowkey is privately shared in a conversation.'
  • 'Highkey def: awesome, or really clutch. Lowkey def: subtle, kinda.'

In addition to having respondents define the words in their own terms, I asked them to rate the following data sets on their likeliness to use them in a conversation.

  1. Highkey, I hate potato chips.
  2. I hate potato chips highkey.
  3. I highkey hate potato chips.
  1. Lowkey, these girls are weird.
  2. These girls are weird lowkey.
  3. These girls are lowkey weird.

The respondents were most likely to use highkey in the third placement, and least likely to place highkey in the second placement. The respondents were most likely to use lowkey in the first placement, and least likely to use lowkey in the second placement. The preference in placement could be related to the opposing meanings of the terms.

Syntax of highkey as an adverb

When testing lowkey against Eric Potsdam's theory in A Syntax for Adverbs, Stanfield and Thomas found that lowkey followed along with Potsdam's predictions. In order to accurately assess the relation between highkey and lowkey, I also tested highkey against Potsdam's proposal that 'Adverb classes are licensed by X̊ heads and must be structurally realized in the government domain of the head' (1999).

When used as an S-adverb licensed by the C head, highkey is placed at the far left of the clause with the 'obvious' interpretation, and it modifies the whole clause and characterizes the utterance as a statement the speaker wishes everyone to know. Lowkey characterizes the whole clause also, but characterizes the utterance as a secretive statement between the speaker and a specific small audience rather than a statement for everyone to hear.

When highkey and lowkey are compared as S-adverbs licensed by the I head, both will cause a change in meaning of audience/level of secrecy when embedded between the subject and verb; however, the change in meaning differs for the two.

  1. Highkey, Adam wants to ask Amanda on a date (obviously)
  2. Adam highkey wants to ask Amanda on a date. (obviously, the subject)
  3. Lowkey, Adam wants to ask Amanda on a date. (secretly)
  4. Adam lowkey wants to ask Amanda on a date. (secretly, the subject)

When used as a VP adverb licensed by the V head, highkey and lowkey both modify the degree of the main verb. Lowkey means 'kinda' while highkey means 'really' or 'absolutely'.

For future research, I am interested in looking at lowkey and highkey being used as verbs, because of a line from the television show Shameless where a character uses lowkey as low keying it: 'I was thinking of low keying it this year.'

Conclusion

Syntactically, the terms act the same and semantically they are opposites of each other. Since they are opposites, at the beginning of the sentence highkey is used to get everyone's attention and lowkey is used as a simple aside between a few people, and later in the sentence highkey means 'really, absolutely' and lowkey means 'kind of'. From the data collected I could not conclude why the preference of placement in the sentence was different for lowkey and highkey, but I would like to make the assumption that this is simply because highkey is a newer term that has not been fully grammaticalized into speech.
